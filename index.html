Chapter 01

The 1950's & Drumpf

US Senate Investigation (1954)

Fred Trump was investigated by the U.S. Senate for profiting off government-backed FHA (Federal Housing Administration) loans by inflating construction and appraisal costs on his housing projects..

Woody Guthrie Criticisms

Folk singer Woody Guthrie lived in Fred Trump’s Beach Haven apartment complex in Brooklyn in the 1950s and wrote a song criticizing "Old Man Trump" for his rental and racial practices, though this was a lyrical protest rather than a formal lawsuit.

No Joint 1950s Lawsuits with Donald

Donald Trump was a child/teenager during the 1950s (born in 1946) and was not involved in lawsuits with his father at that time.

Chapter 02

The 1960's & Drumpf

State Funding Scandals & Investigations

  • New York State Investigation Commission (1966): Following the 1954 federal Senate investigation, Fred Trump faced state-level scrutiny in January 1966. He was subpoenaed and forced to testify before the New York State Investigation Commission.
  • The Allegations: Investigators discovered Fred Trump had heavily inflated construction and equipment costs for the state-funded Trump Village project in Coney Island. For example, he billed the state $21,000 to lease a dump truck that was valued at $3,600 and charged $8,280 for tile scrapers that cost $500. Through these padded expenses, he allegedly pocketed over $598,000 in unspent state funds.
  • The Outcome: No criminal charges were filed, but Fred Trump was forced to return $1.2 million to New York State. The investigation severely damaged his reputation, effectively blocking him from securing future public subsidies for major residential projects.
  • Campaign Finance Scandal (1961): Fred Trump was implicated in a minor public scandal when it was revealed he and a group of state contractors contributed to a secret $25,000 campaign fund for New York City mayoral candidate Robert F. Wagner Jr. After the press exposed the money as an attempt to curry political favor for housing contracts, the mayor returned the funds.

Chapter 03

The 1970's

## The 1973 Lawsuit Overview

  • Filing Date: October 15, 1973 (United States v. Fred C. Trump, Donald Trump, and Trump Management, Inc., Docket 1:73-01529, E.D.N.Y.).
  • Core Allegations: The DOJ charged that the Trumps systematically discriminated against Black applicants by telling them apartments were unavailable while offering leases to white applicants, imposing harsher rental terms, and marking Black applicants' forms with a "C" (for "colored").
  • Defense & Countersuit: Represented by attorney Roy Cohn, Donald Trump denied the charges and filed a $100 million countersuit against the federal government for defamation, which was later dismissed.
  • The Settlement: In June 1975, the parties signed a consent decree requiring the Trumps to furnish weekly lists of vacancies to the Urban League, advertise openings in minority-focused newspapers, and obey fair housing laws without admitting any guilt.
  • Follow-up Action: The DOJ sued the Trumps again in 1978 for continuing racial steering and non-compliance before the case was finally closed in 1982.

1973 federal housing suit

In 1973, Trump was accused by the Justice Department of violations of the Fair Housing Act in the operation of 39 buildings. The department said that black "testers" were sent to more than half a dozen buildings and were denied apartments, but a similar white tester would then be offered an apartment in the same building. The government alleged that Trump's corporation quoted different rental terms and conditions to blacks and made false "no vacancy" statements to blacks for apartments they managed in Brooklyn, Queens, and Staten Island.

Representing Trump, Roy Cohn filed a counter-suit against the government for $100 million, asserting that the charges were irresponsible and baseless. A federal judge threw out the countersuit, calling it a waste of "time and paper". Trump settled the charges out of court in 1975 without admitting guilt, saying he was satisfied that the agreement did not "compel the Trump organization to accept persons on welfare as tenants unless as qualified as any other tenant".

Tony Schwartz, the ghostwriter of Trump's book, The Art of the Deal, said that the housing case was "a classic example" of Trump being "a counterpuncher": someone accuses Trump of doing something horrible, and he "goes back at them with all guns blazing.... And admits nothing." If Trump loses, he will "declare victory".

The corporation was required to send a bi-weekly list of vacancies to the New York Urban League, a civil rights group, and give them priority for certain locations. In 1978 the Trump Organization again was in court for violating terms of the 1975 settlement; Trump denied the charges..
